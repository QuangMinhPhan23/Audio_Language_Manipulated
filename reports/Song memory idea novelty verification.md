# Novelty check overrates both song-memory ideas

The novelty check is right on its central claim: nobody has published either study. Idea A gives audio-language models (text chatbots fitted with an audio input, such as Qwen2-Audio or Music Flamingo) edited versions of famous songs. Idea B prompts open music generators with a song title alone and scores what comes out. But both scores are too generous, and the check points you at the wrong idea. Two pieces of prior work it missed shrink the novelty. "Vision Language Models are Biased" already edits images of famous subjects and shows that adding the subject's name makes models *worse* at seeing the edit ([arXiv 2505.23941](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23941)). "Ghost in the Encoder" (ISMIR 2026) already switches ACE-Step 1.5's planner (the small language model that writes a song plan before audio is made) on and off. With it on, outputs match the original recordings more often, and the authors name this "song-recognition trigger" as their next project ([arXiv 2609.39552](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39552)). Fair scores are about **3–3.5/5 for A as a concept, 2.5–3/5 for B, and 2/5 for the combined framing**, against the check's 4, 3 and "conditionally credible". For you the deciding fact is practical, not academic. A's novel part needs recognisable famous recordings, and the only source you have is YouTube clips that are not cleared under Australian law. B needs no copyrighted input audio at all. Your earlier pilot also found **no popularity effect within the Hot 100** (−0.3 points, 95% range −4.1 to +3.5), and the check's "minimal additions" for both ideas lean on exactly that effect ([Review 2026-10-22](file:///D:/WMM/RESEARCH_REVIEW_2026-10-22.md)). The best move is to keep the text-only title study the review recommended for ISMIR 2027, and add one cheap Idea B piece to it: read what ACE-Step's planner writes when given a famous title. That piece is itself a text-only test. Leave Idea A's famous-song arm alone.

## Nobody has run either study, but the gap is narrower than the check claims

For **Idea A**, a fresh search of papers, preprints, workshop pages and GitHub found no study that plays edited famous songs to audio-language models and tests memory against hearing. That covers stem-swapped or stem-removed songs (a stem is one isolated instrument or vocal track), covers, karaoke versions and remixes, with or without a title ([idea A notes](file:///D:/WMM/research_notes/Song%20memory%20idea%20novelty%20verification/idea_a_prior_work.md)). The only stem-aware benchmark for these models, STEMMA from KAIST's Nam lab, has **no condition that removes, swaps or remixes stems**. Its "hard negatives" are near-miss songs from its catalogue, and its prompts use neutral letters, not titles ([arXiv 2610.11884](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.11884)). Building counterfactual mixes (mixes edited so the right answer is known by construction) from multitrack research sets is therefore still unclaimed.

The design and the main claim already exist in vision, though. VLMs-are-Biased edits 1,392 images of familiar subjects, for example by adding a fourth stripe to the three-stripe Adidas logo. Five models score **100% on the originals and 17.05% on the edits**. Writing the subject's name into the image lowers accuracy by a further **4.49 points on average**; putting it in the prompt lowers it by **4.75 points** (two models tested) ([arXiv 2505.23941](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23941)). A reviewer will call Idea A "VLMs-are-Biased for music" far more readily than the "POPEv2 for music" the check warned about. What VLMs-are-Biased lacks is your remaining room. It has no wrong-name condition, no condition where the model must recognise the subject from the signal alone with no name given, no substitution of one part inside a mixture, and no per-item popularity test. Its accuracy also did not follow the authors' own popularity ordering of domains (animals 2.12%, optical illusions 50.87%) ([vision notes](file:///D:/WMM/research_notes/Song%20memory%20idea%20novelty%20verification/vision_text_analogues.md)). The research subagent scored A at **3.5/5** for this reason.

For **Idea B**, no paper, GitHub issue or forum thread was found that prompts open text-to-music models with a title alone and scores outputs against known song attributes. Nothing was found that clamps ACE-Step 1.5's planner either; to clamp is to overwrite the tempo and key the planner wrote with values you choose, then see what title effect is left ([idea B notes](file:///D:/WMM/research_notes/Song%20memory%20idea%20novelty%20verification/idea_b_prior_work.md)). But Ghost in the Encoder ran ACE-Step 1.5 with its chain-of-thought planner off and on. Chain-of-thought means the planner writes out its reasoning, here a caption plus tempo and key, before the audio model runs. The paper scored outputs with a cover-song detector called CLEWS, which checks whether a new recording matches a known one. With the planner on, the matching original turned up in the detector's top 10 for **0.055** of songs, against **0.022** with it off and **0.005** by chance. The authors write that the planner caption "may function as a song-recognition trigger… We leave systematic investigation of this pathway to future work" ([arXiv 2609.39552](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.39552)). A second 2026 paper already measures how well ACE-Step 1.5 obeys explicit key instructions: it hits the exact key **66.7%** of the time ([arXiv 2608.11899](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.11899)). That is the "renderer obedience" piece of B's proposed three-part breakdown. Still unclaimed are: title-only prompts (Ghost used lyrics, and GEMA used lyrics plus title), reading the planner's written tempo and key and scoring them against the real song, the clamp, and wrong-title controls.

| Idea | Novelty check | Adjusted score | Why it moved |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, edited famous songs | 4/5 | **3–3.5/5** as a concept; about 2.5/5 in the form you can legally run | VLMs-are-Biased owns the design in vision. The legal version (unknown songs with a fake famous title) is a typed-text conflict, which existing audio-text conflict papers already cover |
| B, generators prompted by title | 3/5 | **2.5–3/5** | Ghost already tests the planner pathway and has announced the follow-up; a 2026 paper already measures key obedience |
| A+B, "song memory distorts listening and composing" | Credible main-track paper if it has a popularity dose-response and a mechanism | **About 2/5** for a solo student by May 2027 | Its glue, an effect that grows with fame, failed in your pilot, and A's famous arm is blocked |

Four corrections change what you should do. First, the Ghost error means B's planner component is not untouched; it is an announced next step for a group already working on exactly this model. Second, the check's B design treats "clamp tempo and key" as cutting the planner's influence. It does not. With thinking switched on, ACE-Step's planner also writes 5 Hz **audio semantic codes**, a coarse sketch of the audio, about five tokens per second, that feed the diffusion model. Even with the planner off, captions are encoded by **Qwen3-0.6B**, a language model with its own world knowledge ([ACE-Step INFERENCE.md](https://github.com/ace-step/ACE-Step-1.5/blob/main/docs/en/INFERENCE.md); [Tutorial.md](https://github.com/ace-step/ACE-Step-1.5/blob/main/docs/en/Tutorial.md)). So no ACE-Step setting is free of text knowledge. Third, the check's advice to "post an arXiv preprint of A early (ICASSP 2027…)" is stale: ICASSP 2027's deadline closed in September 2026 ([IEEE SPS](https://signalprocessingsociety.org/events/2027-ieee-international-conference-acoustics-speech-and-signal-processing-icassp)). Fourth, and most important, its advice to "build A first; it carries less risk" is backwards for you, for the legal and data reasons below.

## What the check missed: a vision twin, a planner test, and older popularity findings

Beyond VLMs-are-Biased and Ghost's planner experiment, the check missed a large body of work showing that **"the effect grows with fame" is already established in text and image generation**. If you found it in music, it would be a replication, not a discovery. In text question-answering, models stick with their memorised answer over contradicting context more often for popular facts; GPT-4 does so about 80% of the time for the most popular questions ([Xie et al., arXiv 2305.13300](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.13300)). Accuracy tracks entity popularity, with a correlation of about 0.4 for GPT-3 ([PopQA, arXiv 2212.10511](https://arxiv.org/html/2212.10511)). Memorisation of novels tracks how often they appear on the web, with rank correlations up to 0.84 ([Speak, Memory, arXiv 2305.00118](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.00118)). The entity-swap method for creating knowledge conflicts dates to 2021 ([Longpre et al., arXiv 2109.05052](https://arxiv.org/html/2109.05052v2)). In image generation, Stable Diffusion's extracted training images were mostly ones duplicated 100 or more times ([Carlini et al., arXiv 2301.13188](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.13188v1)). Imitating a face or art style takes roughly 200–700 training images of it ([arXiv 2410.15002](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15002v1)). The vision papers that locate the override inside the model, Visual CounterFact and "Vision-Default, Prior-Override", find that 2.5–4.8% of attention heads drive it. They use everyday object colours, not named famous entities ([arXiv 2505.17127](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.17127); [arXiv 2606.28273](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.28273)).

One image result gives Idea B a ready control. A text-to-image model trained without art reportedly fails to draw the Mona Lisa from its title, while Stable Diffusion 1.4 succeeds. If true, recall from a title comes from the images in training, not from the text encoder alone. This comes from a search summary and has not been checked against the paper ([arXiv 2412.00176](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00176v1)). The music version of that control is MusicGen, which was trained on licensed stock music with the vocals removed ([MusicGen card](https://huggingface.co/facebook/musicgen-small)). Smaller music items to watch: MusicListenBench ("Can audio LLMs hear the difference yet?") has an empty dataset page, so its design is unknown ([GitHub](https://github.com/AMAAI-Lab/MusicListenBench)). An anonymous repository names concept familiarity as a confound in audio-text bias tests, but works only with speech ([GitHub](https://github.com/yunaLee21/audio-text-bias-diagnosis)).

On scoop risk, the subagents' judgments (not data) differ from the check in two ways. For A, the most dangerous group is UMD's Manocha lab working with NVIDIA, through Sreyan Ghosh. He co-wrote both Music Flamingo's famous-song remark and a 2026 paper that swaps real audio tracks under videos to test whether models listen ([arXiv 2604.02605](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.02605)). The VLMBias authors porting their design to audio is also plausible. The check's 35–45% estimate for an A-type paper before May 2027 is reasonable. For B, its 20–30% looks **low for the planner piece specifically**, because the Ghost authors have publicly named it as future work. It is still reasonable for a title-only, attribute-scored study. None of these numbers is sourced. ISMIR 2026 late-breaking demos, Interspeech 2026 and NeurIPS 2026 workshop papers were not checked one by one, so an unpublished workshop paper could exist.

## Law and data block the famous-song half of Idea A

Idea A's novel core, a "recognition-gated" test that only counts songs the model recognises from the audio, needs recordings a model would recognise. **None of the clean research multitrack sets contain charting recordings.** MUSDB18-HQ's 150 tracks come from Cambridge-MT, MedleyDB, a Native Instruments pack and a stems remix contest, by artists such as Al James and Hop Along ([SigSep](https://sigsep.github.io/datasets/musdb.html)). Your own metadata work found most MoisesDB songs "look like library music with no public release" ([CLAUDE.md](file:///D:/WMM/CLAUDE.md)). So the clean arm can only ever be the "unrecognised" control. The famous arm means separating commercial recordings, and that brings two problems.

The first problem is legal. Australia has **no text-and-data-mining exception**; the Attorney-General ruled one out on 26–27 October 2025 ([ACS Information Age](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2025/govt-rules-out-ai-copyright-exemption-for-tech-giants.html)). Your only defence is fair dealing for research or study: s103C covers sound recordings and s40 the underlying musical work. It is judged case by case on five factors, with no safe amount for audio ([ALRC](https://www.alrc.gov.au/publication/copyright-and-the-digital-economy-dp-79/7-fair-dealing/current-law/)). UNSW's library guidance says fair dealing does **not** cover material that will be published ([UNSW copyright guide](https://subjectguides.library.unsw.edu.au/copyright/study_and_research)). Downloading from YouTube breaches its Terms of Service ([YouTube Terms](https://www.youtube.com/static?template=terms)). Defeating YouTube's stream protection may also be circumvention under s116AN, which has no general research exception ([AustLII s116AN](https://20.austlii.edu.au/au/legis/cth/consol_act/ca1968133/s116an.html)). The Munich court in GEMA v. Suno treated stream ripping from YouTube as exactly that kind of circumvention ([KPW](https://kpw.law/en/gema-also-wins-against-suno/)). Making stem-swapped versions adds a further act of reproducing or adapting the recording, which buying the track does not license ([legal notes](file:///D:/WMM/research_notes/Song%20memory%20idea%20novelty%20verification/legal_record_and_risk.md)). A listening study of naturalness, the check's proposed rebuttal to the "artifacts" objection, needs UNSW ethics approval before any data collection ([UNSW procedure](https://unsw.edu.au/content/dam/pdfs/governance/policy/2022-01-policies/humanresearchethicsprocedure.pdf)). It almost certainly also needs a staff Chief Investigator.

The second problem is scientific. Separation quality on the "other" stem (guitar, keys, synth) is only about **7.7–9.0 dB** SDR (signal-to-distortion ratio, a standard measure of how cleanly a separator isolates a source; higher is cleaner) for BS-RoFormer, one of the strongest current separators ([arXiv 2309.02612](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2309.02612)). Demucs's six-stem model has "a lot of bleeding and artifacts" on piano ([Demucs README](https://github.com/facebookresearch/demucs)). A model that still reports a "removed" guitar might be hearing leftover guitar, not remembering one. On top of this, no published benchmark shows open audio-language models can name songs from audio at all. Your own Qwen2-Audio scored 37% on famous-song instrument questions while picking "D" 54% of the time ([Review 2026-10-22](file:///D:/WMM/RESEARCH_REVIEW_2026-10-22.md)). If open models recognise almost no songs, the "recognised" cell is empty and the gate cannot be estimated. What remains legal and cheap is a weaker design. You would edit clean, unknown multitracks with your existing stem-removal pipeline and label them with a false famous title. That is a typed-text conflict, the case the check's own hostile reviewer already calls "a special case of known audio–text conflict".

Idea B has a much easier legal profile. It needs no copyrighted input audio; the open models' licences allow research use (ACE-Step 1.5 is MIT; MusicGen weights are CC-BY-NC); and in GEMA v. Suno, the court placed responsibility for infringing outputs on the model operator, as "systemic causes", not on the person prompting ([Simmons & Simmons](https://www.simmons-simmons.com/en/publications/cmszvaaz6003oug849s2sqawl/gema-v-suno-ai-key-eu-decision-on-ai-training-outputs-and-copyright)). Compute is small too: ACE-Step makes a full song in under 2 seconds on an A100, and its 0.6B–4B planners fit on any shared GPU ([ACE-Step README](https://github.com/ace-step/ACE-Step-1.5)). The risk sits in publishing. Posting generated audio that resembles famous songs is distribution, and research fair dealing does not cover it.

## Fold a planner readout into the text-only title study

The external review recommended a text-only study for ISMIR 2027, based on its finding that the right song name adds **+15.5 points** over the year alone (95% range 10.2 to 21.0). The question is how much a song's name changes what language models say about its sound, beyond year, artist and base rates ([Review 2026-10-22](file:///D:/WMM/RESEARCH_REVIEW_2026-10-22.md)). The useful insight is that **ACE-Step's planner is itself a text model**, pretrained from Qwen3 0.6B, 1.7B or 4B ([model card](https://huggingface.co/ACE-Step/Ace-Step1.5)). Reading what it writes when given a title is a text-only identity probe of exactly the kind the review endorsed. Its target is a deployed music generator rather than a chatbot. It needs no audio, so neither the YouTube problem nor the ethics problem arises. And it is the one piece of Idea B that the planner and key-obedience papers have not done. Make it a section of the ISMIR paper, not a second paper: "does a song's name change what a music generator *plans* to make?" You can score the planner's caption against the same MGPHot instrument labels you already use, and its tempo and key against real values where you have a lawful source.

Run a planner-only pilot first: about 400 titles, no audio generated, a few GPU-hours. A 0.6B–4B model may simply not know songs' tempo or key, which is a real risk. A null there is still publishable inside the text-study framing, but it would end any plan to grow B into its own paper. Only if the planner shows a clear title effect should you go on to generate audio. Then use four settings, not the check's two. First, planner off with the title in the caption. Second, planner free, logging its metadata, caption and audio codes. Third, planner metadata clamped to neutral or wrong-title values. Fourth, the wrong-title run's audio codes or caption swapped in. Before trusting any clamp, test in code how a tempo or key you set interacts with the planner's own fields, because the documentation does not say ([idea B notes](file:///D:/WMM/research_notes/Song%20memory%20idea%20novelty%20verification/idea_b_prior_work.md)).

| Control | Why it is needed |
|---|---|
| Year only; true title + artist + year | The review's primary contrast; carries the +15.5-point benchmark into the planner test |
| Same-artist wrong title | Separates song-specific knowledge from artist-level style (review round 2) |
| Wrong title matched on decade, genre and chart band, split by whether the donor song has the attribute | Your pilot showed wrong-name harm appears only when the donor lacks the instrument |
| Descriptive prompt with no title | The music version of the Art-Free control: description-following versus title recall |
| Out-of-sample decade and decade + genre rule | A no-model decade rule (61.0%) beat Claude given the year (54.0%) |
| MusicGen and Stable Audio Open | "Knows the name, probably never heard the song" controls; expect near-null effects from their small T5 encoders |
| Measured tempo of every generated clip, with half/double-tempo folding; several seeds per song | Settings are requests, not guarantees; ACE-Step hits the exact key only about two-thirds of the time |
| Report popularity only as a secondary, pre-stated test | Your pilot null predicts a flat result |

Some claims should stay out of every version. Do not say that naming a song makes generators reproduce it: GEMA v. Suno already found that, with lyrics. Do not say yours is the first study of ACE-Step's planner (Ghost ran one) or that you discovered partial key obedience (2608.11899 did). Do not say ACE-Step was trained on commercial music. Its card claims licensed, royalty-free and synthetic data, and Ghost's "1.8M-song" corpus figure appears to come from ACE-Step v1, not 1.5 ([arXiv 2506.00045](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.00045); [model card](https://huggingface.co/ACE-Step/Ace-Step1.5)). Do not say MusicGen or Stable Audio Open "never heard" the songs; neither paper states that. Do not claim that fame increases the effect, or that planner knowledge proves memory of a recording. It shows text recall, which is the review's point about the original study too. Do not claim "exact" tempo or key, or use legal conclusions such as "infringed" about named developers. Avoid scoring resemblance to the original recordings (Ghost's cover-detector measure): that needs the uncleared clips as references, which takes the study back into the YouTube problem. For the same reason, any tempo ground truth taken from your Katana clips counts as a clip-based result. Clear it with copyright@unsw.edu.au before it goes into a paper. Keep prompts free of lyrics, which also keeps you away from GEMA v. OpenAI ([Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/landmark-ruling-of-the-munich-regional-court-(gema-v-openai)-on-copyright-and-ai-training)). Never post generated audio publicly; release only statistics.

On timing, the next realistic deadlines are Interspeech 2027 on 9 February 2027 ([mldeadlines](https://mldeadlines.com/conference/interspeech-2027/)) and ISMIR 2027, probably late April, though the call is not yet out ([mldeadlines ISMIR](https://mldeadlines.com/conference/ismir/)). A short arXiv preprint of the planner pilot after it runs would timestamp the title-only angle against the Ghost group.

## Conclusion

The novelty check reads the novelty questions well but the decision badly. It ranked the ideas by how new they look on paper. For you, what matters is which experiment you can run legally, alone and by April, and whether its central claim survives your own data. On that test the order flips. Idea A's distinctive piece rests on audio you cannot lawfully use and on a popularity effect your pilot already failed to find. Idea B's distinctive piece is a cheap text probe that fits inside the paper the review already told you to write. The useful reframing is that "song memory" in a music generator can be studied as text knowledge in its planner. That keeps the claim honest about what it shows: what models recall from a name, not what they hear or copy.

Two open questions decide how far to push this. First, does a small Qwen3 planner know anything song-specific beyond decade and genre? Only the pilot can answer that. Second, will UNSW sign off on any clip-derived numbers? If the planner pilot is null, the text-only study stands on its own and you lose a few GPU-hours. If it is strong, you hold the one result in this space that the Ghost group has announced but not yet published, and an early preprint is worth more than a bigger design.
